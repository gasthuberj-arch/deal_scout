Architecture • Lesson 0003

Design It Twice & Grilling the Frontier

How to explore interface alternatives and eliminate silent assumptions before writing code.

Culmination of Your Architecture Mission

In Lesson 0001 and Lesson 0002, you mastered deep modules, seams, and dependency categories. Now, you combine them with two powerful techniques: Design It Twice (divergent exploration) and Grilling (convergent interrogation).

1. Design It Twice: Escaping First-Idea Bias

John Ousterhout observed that a software engineer's first idea for an interface is rarely their best. You tend to latch onto whatever implementation was easiest to imagine first.

In Design It Twice, before writing production code, you intentionally draft 3+ radically different interface variations for the same problem:

  • Design A (Minimalist): Shrinks the interface to 1–2 calls with opinionated defaults.
  • Design B (Configurable): Exposes pluggable middleware or options for maximum extensibility.
  • Design C (Common-Case First): Optimizes ergonomics for the 90% common path, keeping advanced options optional.

Comparing these side-by-side reveals hidden trade-offs in depth, locality, and seam placement before you invest in code.

2. Grilling: Eliminating Silent Assumptions

Once you have a candidate design, how do you prevent costly rework? You grill the plan.

Grilling is systematic interrogation across a design tree to ensure no critical decision or edge case is left silently assumed.

3. The Frontier & Division of Labor

A grilling session proceeds in rigorous rounds governed by two core rules:

  1. The Frontier: The questions you ask in any round must only be those whose prerequisites are already settled. You never ask downstream questions whose answers depend on unsettled upstream choices.
  2. Facts vs. Decisions: Finding facts is the agent's job; making decisions is the user's. If a question requires inspecting a database schema, reading an existing module, or running mix tests, the agent dispatches tools or sub-agents to discover the facts. The user is only asked to make genuine product or architectural decisions.
